The bottom line

Angle offers the best coverage at the lowest cost. Among the PEOs, it's ADP.

Angle Health wins on every measurable axis in year one — better plan designs, lower employee cost, and $12,181 less employer spend than the cheapest PEO. It carries two risks the PEOs don't: a renewal you expect at +40%, and a scope gap (no payroll, HR, workers' comp or EPLI) that nobody has priced. If Onyx wants one vendor for everything, ADP TotalSource is the answer — cheapest all-in, the lowest contribution floor in the field, the only vendor that lets us curate the plan menu, and the single best plan design on any PEO's shelf.

Why ADP beats TriNet

  • $4,586 less in year-one employer cost — and this figure is now firm, since TriNet has confirmed the $1,200/month minimum applies.
  • Floor of $187.02 vs $269.50 — both clear our $300 target, but ADP leaves the most room.
  • Its $2,500-deductible plan caps a member at $3,500 and pays 100% after deductible. TriNet's closest rung has a $6,850 cap. But see the household-by-household comparison — for the two family tiers TriNet's ACO 6500 beats it.
  • ADP's fee is 1.53% of payroll and stays below TriNet's $14,400 floor until payroll reaches ~$941,000.
  • ADP lets us present 3–5 plans. Insperity requires the full menu. TriNet says all twelve are available — whether that obliges us to offer all twelve is not yet confirmed, and it matters less than it looks: offering the cheapest plan is what holds the floor at $269.50.

Why TriNet beats Insperity

  • TriNet is $1,869 cheaper in year one — and Insperity's life and LTD rates were never quoted, so its real figure is higher still.
  • Insperity's cheapest plan — a $6,500 deductible at $604/mo — costs more than TriNet's richest mid-tier plans.
  • Insperity's floor of $509.76 pushes Onyx $2,517 above the contribution target and buys Lisa a $5,000-deductible plan for it.
  • Insperity offers seven rungs to TriNet's twelve, and its two cheapest carry the highest out-of-pocket maximums in the field — $9,200 and $8,500 for an individual.
  • Every Insperity plan costs a family more than $871/mo after Onyx's $1,000 — more than ADP's mid-tier plans charge, on a $6,500 deductible.

Coinsurance is shown as the share the plan pays after the deductible; office-visit, pharmacy and emergency-room figures are what the member pays.

Reading the employee columns. Each cell shows the monthly payroll deduction, then the annual total broken into its two parts: premium (twelve months of deductions) and care (the household's out-of-pocket maximum). These are separate pots of money — an out-of-pocket maximum caps what you pay for treatment and never includes premiums — so they add without overlapping. That is why a total can run two or three times the out-of-pocket maximum: on the richer plans the premium is the larger half. Christine on ADP's OA MC 1000 pays $12,620 in premium against a $9,000 care cap; for Steven or Jessica on that plan premium is 65% of the total.

  • For the two family tiers, TriNet's ACO 6500 is the best plan in the PEO field on both axes — $166/mo, within $6 of ADP's cheapest plan, and a worst case $583 below ADP's best. That single plan is the strongest argument against treating ADP as best for everyone.
  • ADP is decisively best for Christine. Its 2500-100Copay caps her household at $15,457; TriNet's best for her is $18,468 and Insperity's is $22,014 — $6,557 worse.
  • Insperity is best for exactly one person. Lisa's 3000 Primary Advantage is genuinely good value, but only because Onyx is forced to overpay $209.76/month on her behalf. That is the floor working in her favor and against Onyx.
  • Two caveats before anyone elects the ACO 6500. It sits on Banner Health Network only, so provider access is narrower than the rest of TriNet's menu. And worst case is not a typical year: it carries a $13,000 family deductible with office copays before the deductible and 100% coverage after, while ADP's 2500-100Copay has a $5,000 family deductible but applies it to office visits. The ACO 6500 is better for routine care and for a catastrophic year; ADP's plan is better through the middle.
  • Lowest deduction is not lowest risk. Lisa pays $74/mo on ADP's 8000 ValueNT but carries $10,888 of exposure. On Angle's TRAD 2000/4000 she pays $192 and her exposure drops to $6,305 — $4,583 less risk for $1,417 more premium.
  • Angle's richest plan costs a family $694/mo — less than what all three PEOs charge for mid-tier coverage, with a $1,000 deductible instead of $2,500–$5,000.
  • Insperity's low-end number is an illusion. Lisa's $108/mo looks competitive only because the floor forces Onyx to overpay $209.76/month on her behalf, and it buys her a $5,000-deductible plan. Christine and both families pay the highest deductions in the field on that same plan.
  • HSAs are noted, not modeled — by choice. The [HSA] tag marks which plans are HSA-qualified and changes no figure in this report. Worst case is 12 months of payroll deductions plus the household out-of-pocket maximum, with no employer seed, no employee contribution and no credit for the tax deduction on HSA dollars. We cannot know what any individual will choose to contribute, and building an assumption about that into the numbers would hide the guess rather than remove it. The effect is that these figures overstate the true cost of the HDHP options relative to the copay plans, since HDHP spending can be funded pre-tax and copay-plan spending cannot. Two things worth knowing when reading the HDHP rows: ADP's 8000 ValueNT is not HSA-eligible — it pays $10 primary-care and Tier 1 copays before the deductible — so its $10,000 exposure has no pre-tax offset at all; and on every plan here the family out-of-pocket maximum exceeds one year's HSA contribution limit, so the offset is partial in year one and depends on accumulated balance.
  • Out-of-network is ADP's one structural weakness — nine of its eleven plans cover nothing out of network. We have judged this immaterial: no out-of-state work travel, and Arizona has adequate in-network access. One national plan is kept in the lineup regardless.

Not applicable at five employees

Several headline inclusions carry no internal value for Onyx as it stands: EEO-1 reporting (not required below 100 employees), multi-state PTO guidance (Arizona only), international hiring and contractor payments, the 401(k) annual independent audit (not required under 100 participants), and DEI workshops for supervisors. TriNet's proposal also sells an "industry specific solution tailored for nonprofits" and expense tagging to "grants or programs" — recycled content that does not describe Onyx.

One qualification: TriNet is pitching Onyx as a referral partner, so its full catalog has business-development value even where it has no internal use. That is revenue-side, and deliberately excluded from the cost tables.

The structure of that partnership is not a detail. TriNet has offered it two ways: money paid back to Onyx, or a discount passed to the client. Money back to Onyx is a referral fee, and for a CPA firm that engages the AICPA Code of Professional Conduct — commissions and referral fees are prohibited from any client for whom we perform an audit, review, or a compilation where we are not independent, and permitted only with written disclosure to every other client. The Arizona State Board may impose more. The client-discount version avoids all of it and still makes the referral worth making. Confirm the rule before agreeing to a structure, not after.

Why IT support is not credited to TriNet

  • There is no current spend to offset. Onyx pays nothing for IT today, so Electric reduces a future cost we have not committed to — it is not a saving against ADP.
  • The tier that solves the problem is the priced one. Electric's basic plan is not expected to be sufficient, and upgrade pricing is still open. That is simply buying IT support, which can be bought from anyone.
  • It couples two decisions that should stay separate. Leaving TriNet later would also mean re-contracting IT.
  • Security requirements should drive that choice. As a tax preparer Onyx falls under the FTC Safeguards Rule and needs a written information security plan per IRS Pub 4557. Whether a provider supports that matters more than whether it is bundled with payroll. Confirm the basic tier even qualifies before treating it as a benefit.

Value verdict

ADP on value, and the margin is wider than the soft benefits can close.

TriNet has the deepest documented catalog and four capabilities nobody else offers — Perks, Total Comp Statements, Healthee and TriNetExpense. The fifth, $0 employer 401(k) cost, is no longer exclusive: ADP matches it and adds no participant account fee, so the retirement line no longer narrows ADP's lead. ADP is $4,586 a year cheaper with better plan designs, and that figure now stands without a 401(k) caveat and without a fee caveat — TriNet has confirmed the $1,200 monthly minimum applies. Once IT support is set aside — it is new money either way — TriNet's remaining edge is real but unpriced convenience, not cost. Insperity finishes third: category parity on services, worst coverage, highest floor, and the only 401(k) that bills the employer.

Method and data quality

How these numbers were built

Every bid was re-rated

  • No vendor priced Onyx's actual group. TriNet and Insperity both quoted Elena Nikolov at $41,600 with Jessica absent, on $564,922 of payroll. Angle priced three employee-only and one family, six members. ADP used $628,680 of payroll against our actual $658,322.
  • Every figure here was re-priced by us to actual enrollment — Lisa employee-only, Christine employee+spouse, Steven and Jessica family, Josephine waiving — using each vendor's published tier rates.
  • The model validates against source. It reproduces Insperity's own published employee contributions to the penny: $107.68 on the 5000 HDHP and $134.58 on the 3000 Primary Advantage.

Known gaps, carried not filled

  • Insperity's life and LTD rates were never quoted. Its employer cost is therefore understated. Its medical plan designs, by contrast, are now complete — all seven Saver rungs come from its own Benefits At A Glance.
  • Angle discloses no broker or consulting fee. Its "21% administration" line is the third-party administrator's cost inside the premium, not a fee billed to Onyx. CapFi's own compensation is not stated anywhere in the presentation.
  • Angle's TRAD 2000/4000 tier rates are derived from the ratios in its two documented plans, which reproduce both exactly. Confirm before quoting to employees.
  • TriNet's contribution floor was our derivation and TriNet has now confirmed it — 70% of the minimum funding on the lowest-cost plan offered. Our figure of $269.50 stands, keyed to the ACO 6500's $385 employee-only rate. One thing to verify: TriNet now says twelve plans are available, where the strategy appendix we were given lists sixteen Aetna rungs. If the ACO 6500 is not among the twelve, the floor rises — dropping only the four cheapest would push it to $364.00 and above our $300 employee-only target.
  • Insperity's floor mechanic is now confirmed in writing and it is not what any of the earlier quotes implied. The floor is 75% of the employee-only rate of each package's designated base plan — the 3400 HDHP under Saver ($679.68 → $509.76) and the 2000 Deductible under Select ($862.53 → $646.90) — not 75% of the cheapest plan offered. Insperity also caps the employer contribution at 100% of the elected plan's premium, which is why a floor above a plan's full cost is not the impossibility it first appeared to be.
